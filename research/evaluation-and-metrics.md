# Evaluating a generated solo: metrics, human baselines, listening tests

How the research literature measures machine-generated jazz, what real players
actually score, and which failure modes listeners detect. This is the file to
consult before claiming a change made the solo better.

## The headline: we are too consonant

Published baselines for **real** jazz solos:

| Source | Corpus | Chord tones | Colour tones / scale | Chromatic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BebopNet (ISMIR 2020) | 284 transcribed solos — Parker, Stitt, Adderley, Gordon, Rollins, Getz, Woods, Ammons | **50–54%** chord match | 78–83% scale match | — |
| JazzGAN (MUME 2018) | 44 leadsheets, ~1700 bars | **56%** | 34% colour | 4% approach + 6% clash |

Every one of the eight saxophonists BebopNet measured sits between 50% and 54%.
Our generator sits at **66%**, with 27% tensions and 7% chromatic — roughly ten
points more chord tones and seven points fewer colour tones than the human
corpus. See [solo-baseline-measurements.md](solo-baseline-measurements.md).

Being *more* harmonically correct than a human is not a virtue, and the
literature says so twice. BebopNet's authors note they do not expect a perfect
harmonic match, because chromatic lines, approach notes and enclosures are the
language. And in a melody-harmonization study, Yeh et al. found template and HMM
models scoring **above** the human baseline on chord-tone ratio and read that as
the models being "too conservative" — the giveaway, not the goal.

GenJam makes the point most sharply. Its representation resolves pitch only at
play time, by looking the contour up in a scale suggested by the current chord,
so Biles can write that it **makes all the changes because it only plays
theoretically correct notes**. It still doesn't sound human. Harmonic
correctness is trivially achievable and nowhere near sufficient.

**Target the human value, never the maximum.**

## What listeners actually detect

The most relevant published experiment is Frieler & Zaddach, *TISMIR* 2020 — an
actual jazz Turing test, open access at
<https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/10.5334/tismir.87>.

41 jazz-affine participants, 29 classed as experts, 14 solos, ten 7-point items
including "do you think the notes of this solo were generated by a computer
algorithm?"

| | identifying algorithms | identifying humans |
|---|---|---|
| Experts | 64.4% | 53.6% |
| Non-experts | 41.7% | 44.7% |

Only 4 of 14 solos had confidence intervals excluding chance. Non-experts are at
chance in both directions. A hand-edited algorithmic solo fooled the panel
outright.

Two findings from that paper should shape anything we build:

1. **Rendering matters as much as note content.** The same solo scored 5.69 for
   liking as original audio and 3.51 as a MIDI-rendered sax — a gap of 2.19 on a
   7-point scale. Their reading is that timbre, articulation, micro-timing and
   band interaction are at least as important as which notes were chosen. This is
   worth remembering before spending all our effort on the note generator: the
   Real sample pack and the swing/lag work may buy more perceived quality than
   melodic sophistication does.
2. **People report dislike, not machine-detection.** Liking correlates with
   correct identification at r = .86 for human solos and r = −.66 for computer
   solos. Bluntly: solos by non-professional humans were rated worse on average
   than the algorithmic ones. A naive discrimination test mostly measures whether
   people enjoyed the music.

Their rating instrument is worth reusing rather than reinventing — ten items
reducing to two factors that explain 85% of variance: **MUSICALITY** (convincing,
liking, expressive, swing, inventive, expertise) and **COMPLEXITY** (virtuosic,
complex), plus rhythmic exactness on its own.

Pachet & Roy's ["(Manufac)Turing Tests for Music"](https://www.francoispachet.fr/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/pachet-15c.pdf)
(AAAI 2015 workshop) is the citation for preferring metrics over Turing tests. Its
six obstacles include that music has no common-sense baseline, that it is
multi-dimensional so a great melody can fail on poor rendering, that listeners
carry a bias against anything labelled computer-generated, and that assessment
cost grows linearly with the variety you are trying to demonstrate.

## Documented failure modes

Ordered roughly by how much they apply to us.

| Failure mode | Evidence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| No motivic development or variation | Authors' own stated limitation: locally coherent, lacking motif development | BebopNet, ISMIR 2020 |
| No long-term structure | Machine self-similarity decays 0.27 → 0.12 across timescales while **human stays flat at ~0.35–0.36** | Jazz Transformer, ISMIR 2020 |
| No breathing between phrases | Adding explicit phrase events improved pitch-entropy and short-range structure metrics | Jazz Transformer |
| Over-consonance | Models scoring above the human chord-tone baseline judged "too conservative" | Yeh et al., *JNMR* |
| Clashes landing on the chord change | Stated as a plausible hypothesis, never measured — an open question | JazzGAN |
| Repeated-note stuttering | Consecutive-pitch repetition 5–8× the human corpus rate | JazzGAN |
| Aimless contour, implausible leaps | Large-leap rate 0.22 vs corpus 0.001 | JazzGAN |
| Drifting off the beat | On-beat rate 0.002–0.06 vs corpus 0.97 | JazzGAN |
| Inconsistent groove | Groove consistency 0.69–0.76 vs human 0.86 | Jazz Transformer |
| Over-syncopation | Markov rhythm produced too many syncopated, inhomogeneous rhythms; fixed by forcing a 2:1 on-beat/off-beat ratio | Frieler & Zaddach |
| Dead rendering | Liking drops 2.19/7 when the same solo is rendered as plain MIDI | Frieler & Zaddach |

The self-similarity finding is the cleanest human/machine signature in the
literature, and it lines up exactly with our own measurement: our motif
recurrence is 2%.

## Metrics worth adopting

Cheap, no dependencies, and each has a published human baseline:

- **Four-colour harmonic consistency** — chord tone / colour tone / approach tone
  / outside. Originally Impro-Visor's taxonomy; JazzGAN adopted it unmodified.
  This is what our lab page already displays. Human corpus: 56 / 34 / 4 / 6.
- **CTnCTR** (Yeh et al.) — a resolution-aware chord-tone ratio: a non-chord tone
  only counts against you if the following note is *not* within two semitones.
  Human baseline 0.74. Needs no vocabulary file, so it is cheaper than the
  four-colour scheme and rewards proper approach-note behaviour, which is
  precisely what we lack.
- **Consecutive-pitch repetition, large-leap rate, on-beat rate** (JazzGAN) — pure
  sequence statistics with corpus baselines.
- **Self-similarity across timescales** (Jazz Transformer) — the structural
  measure. Human ~0.35 flat; machines decay. Library at
  <https://github.com/slSeanWU/MusDr>.

An important caution from the Jazz Transformer work: every metric got *worse* as
training loss went down past a point. Lower loss did not mean better music.

## Reusable data and licensing

- **Impro-Visor `vocab/My.voc`** — ~9,000 lines, machine-readable, C-relative and
  auto-transposed. Per chord it gives the spelling, colour tones, chord-tone
  priority, **explicit approach-note groups** (`(target approacher …)`), avoid
  notes, substitutes, and a chord-scale list. This is the single richest free
  data source found for exactly the annotations we want to surface.
  ⚠ **Licence is GPL-2.0 (API-confirmed).** If that is v2-only rather than "v2 or
  later" it is *incompatible* with our GPLv3. Verify before copying the file. The
  ideas — the four categories, approach-note taxonomy, rectification — are not
  copyrightable.
- **Weimar Jazz Database** — 456 human solos, 78 performers, 1925–2009; 200,809
  tone events, 15,402 mid-level units, 80,123 pattern atoms, with categories for
  scale runs, approaches, trills, arpeggios and repetitions. Distributed as
  SQLite + MIDI. ⚠ Licence is stated as ODbL on the official download page while
  some secondary sources say CC BY 4.0 — resolve before use. Workable alongside
  GPLv3 but constrains derived data.
- **BebopNet's corpus is not redistributable** (purchased transcriptions); the
  repo requires you to bring your own.

## GenJam's mutation operators

Worth recording separately because they are exactly the capability BebopNet says
it lacks and our audit says we lack. Instead of bit-flips, GenJam mutates melodic
ideas with **transposition, inversion, retrograde, sorting, smoothing and
sequencing** — a ready-made motivic-development operator set, trivially
implementable, and directly applicable to our motif restatement code.

Its trading design is also instructive: it maps the human's last four bars into
its own representation, mutates, and plays back something *related but different*,
because parroting "would be annoyingly predictable."

### Not verified

Several canonical Impro-Visor grammar papers (Keller & Morrison SMC 2007;
Gillick, Tang & Keller SMC 2009; Yaconelli & Keller CSMC 2018 on motif discovery)
were unreachable — dead conference sites and expired certificates. The
ImproteK/OMax/DYCI2 lineage sits behind a bot challenge on HAL and was not read
at all; the working hypothesis, untested, is that it has no psychometric
listening test, only expert testimony. The Continuator, contrary to common
belief, never published a discrimination test — its evaluation is testimonial.
